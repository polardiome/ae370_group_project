\section{Premise and equations}
Satellites orbiting Earth are affected by atmospheric drag, Earth's nonspherical gravitational field, and the Moon's gravity. These effects can change a satellite's trajectory and require corrective maneuvers to maintain its orbit. We will numerically study these changes and estimate the required velocity corrections.

We will address the following questions with matched simulations at two altitudes:
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}
\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item How does atmospheric drag affect the satellite's orbital trajectory over time?
\item How does Earth's gravitational field, including the $J_2$ perturbation, affect the satellite's orbital trajectory?
\item How does the Moon's gravitational influence change the satellite's trajectory, and how does it compare with drag and $J_2$ at different altitudes?
\item How do the numerical method and time step affect the predicted trajectory, particularly when lunar gravity is included?
\item How much additional corrective thrust is required to maintain the desired orbit when the Moon's gravity is included?
\end{enumerate}

The goal of this project is to understand how atmospheric drag, Earth's gravitational field, and the Moon's gravity can change the satellite motion at different altitudes. In addition to that, we need to see how these impacts affect the correction needed in order to keep the orbit intact.

In order to understand these problems, we will consider the position and velocity of the satellite in an initial value problem (IVP). The entire system is nonlinear and time-dependent as the gravity force is dependent on the position, the atmosphere resistance force is dependent on the altitude and the velocity and the position of the moon is dependent on the time. Because of the lack of a closed form solution for the entire system, we will use a numerical solution to find the motion of the satellite.

Let $\vect r=(x,y)^T$, $\vect v=(v_x,v_y)^T$, $r=|\vect r|$, and $h=r-R_E$. In an Earth-centered inertial frame, the state is
\[
\vect u=(x,y,v_x,v_y)^T,\qquad
\dot{\vect u}=f(t,\vect u)=
\begin{bmatrix}\vect v\\\vect a_g+\vect a_d+\vect a_{J_2}+\vect a_M\end{bmatrix}.
\]
The orbit is equatorial, with $z=0$. Central gravity and the equatorial $J_2$ correction are
\[
\vect a_g=-\frac{\mu_E}{r^3}\vect r,\qquad
\vect a_{J_2}=-\frac{3J_2\mu_E R_E^2}{2r^5}\vect r.
\]
We will assume a stationary atmosphere, so its relative velocity is $\vect v$:
\[
\rho(h)=\rho_0 e^{-(h-h_0)/H},\qquad
\vect a_d=-\frac{C_D A\rho(h)}{2m}|\vect v|\vect v.
\]
The prescribed lunar orbit and third-body acceleration are
\[
\vect r_M(t)=D\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\omega_M t)\\\sin(\omega_M t)\end{bmatrix},
\qquad \omega_M=\frac{2\pi}{T_M},
\]
\[
\vect a_M=\mu_M\left[
\frac{\vect r_M-\vect r}{|\vect r_M-\vect r|^3}
-\frac{\vect r_M}{D^3}\right].
\]
The second lunar term subtracts the Moon's acceleration of Earth's center. Omitting it would give the wrong Earth-centered equations. The lunar phase will start at zero. We will neglect the Sun, atmospheric rotation, and changes in satellite mass. Maneuvers will be modeled as velocity jumps between ODE integration intervals, as described in Section 6.

We will use initial altitudes of $300$ km (low) and $35{,}786$ km (high). Each uncontrolled comparison will start with $\vect u_0=(r_0,0,0,\sqrt{\mu_E/r_0})^T$, where $r_0=R_E+h_{\rm initial}$. This is circular for central gravity alone. It will deliberately be the same initial state in every physical model, so it need not be circular after $J_2$ is added.

From Lecture 10, integrate the ODE across a step:
\[
\vect u(t_{k+1})-\vect u(t_k)=\int_{t_k}^{t_{k+1}} f(t,\vect u(t))\,dt.
\]
Approximating this integral with staged slopes gives the classical RK4 method from Lecture 10:
\begin{align*}
 k_1&=f(t_k,\vect u_k),&
 k_2&=f(t_k+\dt/2,\vect u_k+\dt k_1/2),\\
 k_3&=f(t_k+\dt/2,\vect u_k+\dt k_2/2),&
 k_4&=f(t_k+\dt,\vect u_k+\dt k_3),\\
 \vect u_{k+1}&=\vect u_k+\frac{\dt}{6}(k_1+2k_2+2k_3+k_4).
\end{align*}
Each step will evaluate these four slopes in order and then update the state. The time-dependent Moon position will be recomputed at each stage time.

\subsection*{Deriving the RK4 weights}
We keep Lecture 10's stage choices: two midpoint predictions using half steps,
followed by an endpoint prediction using a full step. To derive the weights,
write the update as $u_{k+1}=u_k+\dt(b_1k_1+b_2k_2+b_3k_3+b_4k_4)$.
Following the Taylor-matching approach of Lecture 11, apply these stages to
$\dot u=\lambda u$ and set $w=\lambda\dt$. Successive substitution gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
 k_1&=\lambda u_k, & k_2&=\lambda u_k(1+w/2),\\
 k_3&=\lambda u_k(1+w/2+w^2/4), &
 k_4&=\lambda u_k(1+w+w^2/2+w^3/4).
\end{aligned}
\]
The weighted update therefore becomes
\[
\frac{u_{k+1}}{u_k}
=1+(b_1+b_2+b_3+b_4)w
+\left(\frac{b_2+b_3}{2}+b_4\right)w^2
+\left(\frac{b_3}{4}+\frac{b_4}{2}\right)w^3
+\frac{b_4}{4}w^4.
\]
Match this polynomial to the exact multiplier
$e^w=1+w+w^2/2+w^3/6+w^4/24+O(w^5)$.
Starting with the highest power makes the coefficients easy to solve:
\begin{align*}
 w^4:&\quad b_4/4=1/24 &&\Longrightarrow b_4=1/6,\\
 w^3:&\quad b_3/4+b_4/2=1/6 &&\Longrightarrow b_3=1/3,\\
 w^2:&\quad (b_2+b_3)/2+b_4=1/2 &&\Longrightarrow b_2=1/3,\\
 w^1:&\quad b_1+b_2+b_3+b_4=1 &&\Longrightarrow b_1=1/6.
\end{align*}
Thus the weights are $(1,2,2,1)/6$, as used above. This calculation derives
the weights for the chosen stages and checks fourth order on the linear
test equation. A general nonlinear order proof requires additional Taylor
terms; here we use the classical RK4 method from Lecture 10 and check its
accuracy on the orbital equations by time-step refinement.

We will use RK4 as our main numerical method because it is a good balance between accuracy, stability, and computational costs. RK4 is a fourth order method, and Heun is a second order method, so RK4 has less errors for the same time step. Although RK4 has four function evaluations per step compared with two for Heun, its better accuracy makes the additional costs worthwhile. RK4 has a more suitable stability region for oscillatory problems, and Heun can introduce numerical growth for imaginary eigenvalues. We need a method that can accurately capture differences over several days. Therefore, we will use RK4 for our main simulations and Heun for comparison.

For comparison, Heun uses $k_1=f(t_k,\vect u_k)$ and a forward Euler prediction $\vect u^*=\vect u_k+\dt k_1$, followed by
\[
\vect u_{k+1}=\vect u_k+\frac{\dt}{2}[k_1+f(t_k+\dt,\vect u^*)].
\]
It costs two evaluations per step, compared with four for RK4. Taylor expansion gives
$f(t+\dt,u+\dt f)=f+\dt(f_t+f_u f)+O(\dt^2)$.
Thus Heun matches $u(t+\dt)=u+\dt f+\dt^2(f_t+f_u f)/2+O(\dt^3)$: its one-step error is $O(\dt^3)$ and global order is two for a smooth problem over a fixed interval. RK4 has one-step error $O(\dt^5)$ and global order four, as covered in Lecture 10.

Following Lecture 11, truncation error is the residual after substituting the exact solution, divided by $\dt$. For RK4 this is $O(\dt^4)$, rather than the undivided $O(\dt^5)$ step error. The numerical checks below test the expected global orders on the nonlinear orbital equations.

\section{Stability and independent validation}
For the lecture test equation $\dot u=\lambda u$, let $w=\dt\lambda$.
Using the slopes and weights derived above gives $u_{k+1}=R(w)u_k$, where
\[
R_{\rm RK4}(w)=1+w+\frac{w^2}{2}+\frac{w^3}{6}+\frac{w^4}{24},
\quad R_{\rm Heun}(w)=1+w+\frac{w^2}{2}.
\]
RK4 matches $e^w$ through degree four. Its test-equation truncation error is
$\tau_k=\lambda^5u(t_k)\dt^4/120+O(\dt^5)$. This verifies the order on the linear test problem; it alone is not a proof for every nonlinear ODE.

Absolute stability requires $|R(w)|<1$ for decay. On the imaginary axis, writing $w=iq$ gives $|R_{\rm RK4}(iq)|^2=1-q^6/72+q^8/576$. Thus RK4 has no amplification for $|q|\le\sqrt8$. Heun instead gives $1+q^4/4>1$ for nonzero $q$, which can cause growth in oscillatory calculations. Stability does not guarantee small phase or position error.

The curves bound the regions containing small negative real $w$. Our orbit is nonlinear and time dependent, so a fixed scalar test does not establish its full stability. We do not claim stiffness simply because the Moon and satellite have different periods. RK4 is a practical explicit choice, with reliability checked by refinement.

For independent validation, remove drag, $J_2$, and the Moon. At $300$ km the circular solution is
\[
\vect r(t)=r_0(\cos nt,\sin nt)^T,\qquad
n=\sqrt{\mu_E/r_0^3},\qquad P=2\pi/n=5431.18\ \mathrm{s}.
\]
We will compare over three periods using $E=\max_k|\vect r_k-\vect r(t_k)|$. The known solution checks correctness as well as convergence. We will also check the specific energy $\epsilon=|\vect v|^2/2-\mu_E/r$ and specific angular momentum $\ell=xv_y-yv_x$, which must be constant in this special case. At the finest RK4 step, their maximum relative changes are $1.49\times10^{-12}$ and $7.45\times10^{-13}$.

\section{Physical results over seven days}
We will compare central gravity alone, central gravity with drag, central gravity with $J_2$, and the baseline containing all three. A fifth run will add the Moon to the baseline. No corrections will be applied in this section.
\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=.92\textwidth]{figures/earth_effects.pdf}
\end{center}
\vspace{-8pt}
\textbf{Figure 2.} Differences from central gravity alone. The left panels show signed altitude differences; the right panels show position-separation magnitudes. Curves can overlap.

With drag alone, the low orbit loses $16.643$ km of altitude by day seven. Drag opposes velocity: $\vect v\cdot\vect a_d=-C_D A\rho|\vect v|^3/(2m)\le0$, so it removes orbital energy. The high-orbit density is effectively zero in this exponential model and its drag-only run agrees with central gravity to numerical precision. Extending one density law over this full altitude range is a modeling simplification.

Adding $J_2$ alone gives bounded radial oscillations, reaching $19.785$ km below the initial low altitude and $3.134$ km below the initial high altitude. The initial central-gravity circular speed is too small for a circular orbit in the stronger equatorial gravity field. These oscillations therefore do not mean that $J_2$ dissipates energy. The central-gravity energy formula is not the full conserved energy after adding $J_2$. The altered orbital phase also produces large position separations. Our planar model does not describe changes in the orbital plane.

With drag and $J_2$ together, the low altitude reaches a maximum departure of $38.542$ km from its initial value, ending at $270.427$ km. The combined behavior is not a simple sum: lower altitude increases the assumed density and hence the drag. At high altitude the baseline coincides with the $J_2$-only result.

We will also run Heun on the full lunar model, comparing it with half-step RK4. At low altitude, its maximum position difference falls from $528.5$ km at $5$ s to $98.19$ km at $2.5$ s. At high altitude it falls from $209.9$ km at $120$ s to $49.91$ km at $60$ s. These errors exceed the lunar position signal. A smooth-looking trajectory at these steps would therefore be insufficient evidence of accuracy; method choice matters even when the run does not fail.

The half-step differences in Section 4 are below these signals. However, that only addresses integration error. It does not remove uncertainty caused by the circular Moon orbit, chosen initial phase, or simplified atmosphere. The results apply to this seven-day experiment rather than every satellite or lunar geometry.

\section{A simple orbit-maintenance experiment}
Every $600$ s we will reset velocity while leaving position continuous. Let $r_0$ be the initial radius, $\vect e_r=\vect r/r$, and $\vect e_t=(-y,x)^T/r$. We will prescribe
\[
v_c(r)=\sqrt{\frac{\mu_E}{r}\left[1+\frac32 J_2\left(\frac{R_E}{r}\right)^2\right]},
\qquad
\vect v^+=v_c(r)\vect e_t-\frac{r-r_0}{3600\ \mathrm{s}}\vect e_r.
\]
The circular speed follows by setting $v_c^2/r$ equal to the magnitude of central-plus-$J_2$ radial gravity. The radial term points back toward the initial altitude. The checking interval and return time are fixed assumptions; no optimization or advanced control method is used. At each correction,
\[
\Delta v_j=|\vect v^+-\vect v^-|,\qquad
\Delta v_{\rm total}=\sum_j\Delta v_j.
\]
We will use the same rule in the baseline and lunar runs. There will be $1008$ corrections over seven days, including the correction at the final time. The rule maintains altitude, not a specified longitude or orbital phase. We will use a $3$ km altitude band as a simple success criterion, checking the sampled trajectory between maneuvers as well as at them.

\section{Answers to the project questions}
The answers below follow the numbering in the premise.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Atmospheric drag.} Drag removes orbital energy and lowers the low orbit. With drag alone, its altitude falls by $16.643$ km in seven days. Drag is negligible at the high altitude under the assumed density law (Section 5, Figure 2).

\item \textbf{Earth's gravity and $J_2$.} Central gravity alone maintains the initial circular orbit. Adding $J_2$ strengthens equatorial gravity, changes the orbital phase, and produces radial oscillations because the same initial speed is no longer circular. Maximum altitude departures with $J_2$ alone are $19.785$ km in the low orbit and $3.134$ km in the high orbit. This is not drag-like energy loss (Section 5, Figure 2).

\item \textbf{Lunar gravity.} Relative to the baseline, the Moon produces maximum position separations of $0.4974$ km and $55.457$ km in the low and high orbits. Maximum altitude differences are $5.484$ m and $3.65995$ km. Thus the lunar radial effect is much smaller than drag and $J_2$ at low altitude, but comparable to the $J_2$ radial excursion at high altitude, where drag is negligible (Section 5, Figure 3).

\item \textbf{Method and time step.} Independent validation gives convergence orders of $2.03$ for Heun and $4.10$ for RK4. With the Moon included, halving Heun's step reduces its maximum position difference from the fine RK4 reference from $528.5$ to $98.19$ km in the low orbit and from $209.9$ to $49.91$ km in the high orbit. The selected RK4 steps give coarse--fine position differences of only $1.3773$ m and $0.3185$ m. Method choice and refinement are therefore necessary to distinguish lunar effects from numerical error (Sections 4--5).

\item \textbf{Corrective maneuvers.} For the same maintenance rule, adding the Moon changes the seven-day total $\Delta v$ by $-0.09760$ m/s in the low orbit and $+3.42400$ m/s in the high orbit. Both controlled lunar runs stay within the selected $3$ km altitude band at sampled times (Section 6, Figure 4). These are impulse costs, not engine forces: force would also require specifying burn durations. The negative low-orbit difference means that the Moon partly offsets other deviations for this phase and rule; it is not a general reduction in maintenance cost.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Limits of these answers}
Halving the step changes the signed maneuver-cost differences by $1.15\times10^{-6}$ m/s at low altitude and $1.23\times10^{-5}$ m/s at high altitude, well below the reported changes. These checks address integration error, not physical-model uncertainty. The results use one lunar phase, a prescribed circular Moon orbit, a simplified atmosphere, and fixed satellite parameters. The planar model cannot describe orbital-plane changes. The impulse totals include adjustment from the initial central-gravity circular orbit to the $J_2$ model and are not optimal station-keeping costs.
